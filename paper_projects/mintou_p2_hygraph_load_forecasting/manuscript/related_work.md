Three research threads frame this study: the deep-architecture lineage of short-term and 24-hour-ahead load forecasting, forecasting models that exploit cross-series or graph structure over multiple load series, and the more recent line of work questioning whether architectural complexity in time-series forecasting survives honest evaluation against simple baselines. CSA-LoadNet draws its mechanism from the second thread, but its evaluation discipline—and the shape of its claims—comes from the third.

## Deep Architectures for Short-Term and 24-Hour-Ahead Load Forecasting

Recurrent models are an established deep-learning line in the load domain. Building on the LSTM cell of [Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997], [Kong et al., 2019] demonstrated LSTM-based short-term forecasting at the residential scale, and DeepAR [Salinas et al., 2020] trained one autoregressive recurrent model across a large panel of related series—an idea reflected in CSA-LoadNet's shared temporal encoder. [Lara-Benítez et al., 2020] compared temporal convolutional networks with recurrent alternatives on energy-related series. The Temporal Fusion Transformer [Lim et al., 2021] combined recurrent processing with interpretable attention for multi-horizon prediction, and Informer [Zhou et al., 2021] introduced sparse attention for long sequences. The cited recent applied-load papers use hybrid models: three-channel LSTM–CNN fusion [Zhao et al., 2025], BiLSTM–Transformer dynamic fusion [Xu et al., 2026], an SBOA–SVMD–TCN–BiLSTM pipeline [Yang et al., 2024], and an LSTM–XGBoost ensemble [Dakheel and Çevik, 2025]. In this four-paper sample, component evidence is primarily reported through accuracy comparisons and ablations; the sample does not establish field-wide frequencies of multi-seed or multiplicity-corrected component inference.

## Cross-Series and Graph-Structured Load Forecasting

A second thread asks how a forecaster should use correlated regional or nodal series. LSTNet [Lai et al., 2018] mixes convolutional and recurrent channels over multivariate series. STGCN uses graph convolution over a predefined traffic-sensor graph [Yu et al., 2018], while MTGNN learns graph structure jointly with multivariate forecasting [Wu et al., 2020]. The cited power-load examples cover tensor graph convolution in LoadSeer [Zhang et al., 2024], a GCN–BiLSTM–Adaboost stack [Li et al., 2025], a transformer spatio-temporal graph model [Zhou et al., 2025], and GCN–Transformer integration [Wu et al., 2025]. Hierarchical forecasting instead reconciles series to aggregation constraints [Hyndman et al., 2011; Wickramasuriya et al., 2019]. The Ausgrid source provides customer load and rooftop-PV measurements [Ratnam et al., 2017], but does not itself establish this paper's constructed hierarchy as a standard testbed. A learnable-adjacency appliance study includes fixed-graph controls [Li et al., 2025b], and a smart-meter STGNN benchmark reports graph benefit at residential but not aggregate level [Nguyen et al., 2025]. Those nearby designs rule out an absence or priority claim. The motivation here is narrower: test selected weighting against informative equal averaging under this paper's frozen protocol.

## Simple Baselines and Honest Evaluation

The third thread supplies evaluation discipline. [Tashman, 2000] reviews out-of-sample forecast testing. [Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012] shows that cross-validation validity depends on the predictor and serial dependence, so ordinary random-fold practice is not automatically valid. [Hyndman and Koehler, 2006] documents failures of common accuracy measures, including percentage-error problems near zero, and [Diebold and Mariano, 1995] provides a test of predictive-accuracy differences. [Makridakis et al., 2018] compares statistical and machine-learning forecasters on M3, while the M4 report [Makridakis et al., 2020] evaluates 61 methods over 100,000 series. [Zeng et al., 2023] introduces simple linear baselines; DLinear decomposes a series before linear projection and outperforms the compared transformers on that paper's long-horizon benchmarks. [Hewamalage et al., 2023] consolidates guidance on partitioning, measures, competitive benchmarks, leakage, and statistical testing. TFB broadens method and dataset coverage under a consistent pipeline [Qiu et al., 2024]. These sources motivate strong baselines and scoped inference; they do not establish that component-level testing is novel.

## Gap Statement

The targeted map identifies a comparison need, not a novelty result. Existing load-graph papers vary in task, graph definition, horizon, baseline set, and evaluation design, so their tables cannot answer this paper's matched component question. On the accepted eight-block OPSD analysis, cross-series aggregation is unresolved against the capacity-matched target-self control (MAPE Holm-adjusted p = 0.984); uniform, Euclidean, and fixed-scale weighting comparisons also do not establish a learned-weight advantage. A separate fixed split favored CSA-LoadNet over selected MLP and a smaller-head TemporalOnly arm, but that result is split-conditional and does not override the matched rolling-origin null. Model capacities and training exposure are not uniformly matched in the other scoped datasets. No curvature, GCN, HGCN, priority, or state-of-the-art claim follows from this literature map.
