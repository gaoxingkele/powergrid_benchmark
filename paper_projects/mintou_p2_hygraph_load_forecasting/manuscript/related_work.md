Three research threads frame this study: the deep-architecture lineage of short-term and day-ahead load forecasting, forecasting models that exploit cross-series or graph structure over multiple load series, and the more recent line of work questioning whether architectural complexity in time-series forecasting survives honest evaluation against simple baselines. CSA-LoadNet draws its mechanism from the second thread, but its evaluation discipline — and the shape of its claims — comes from the third.

## Deep Architectures for Short-Term and Day-Ahead Load Forecasting

Recurrent models were the first deep architectures to displace classical statistical forecasters in the load domain. Building on the LSTM cell of [Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997], [Kong et al., 2019] demonstrated LSTM-based short-term forecasting at the difficult residential scale, and probabilistic sequence-to-sequence variants such as DeepAR [Salinas et al., 2020] generalized the recipe to large panels of related series by training one shared network across all of them — an idea CSA-LoadNet inherits in its shared temporal encoder. Convolutional alternatives followed: [Lara-Benítez et al., 2020] showed that temporal convolutional networks match or beat recurrent models on energy-related series at lower training cost. Attention-based architectures then reshaped the field's upper end. The Temporal Fusion Transformer [Lim et al., 2021] combined recurrent processing with interpretable multi-head attention for multi-horizon prediction, and Informer [Zhou et al., 2021] introduced sparse attention to push transformer forecasting toward long sequences. The applied load-forecasting literature in this journal's orbit continues to elaborate this lineage through hybridization: three-channel LSTM–CNN fusion [Zhao et al., 2025], BiLSTM–Transformer combinations with dynamic adaptive fusion [Xu et al., 2026], decomposition-plus-hybrid pipelines such as SBOA–SVMD–TCN–BiLSTM [Yang et al., 2024], and LSTM–XGBoost ensembles for smart-grid load [Dakheel and Çevik, 2025]. Two features of this line are relevant here. First, its dominant move is *component accretion* — each paper adds one or more modules to an already-hybrid stack and reports an accuracy table in which the full stack wins. Second, the evidence for the added component is almost always the accuracy delta of that single table: multi-seed distributions, per-component hypothesis tests, and corrections for multiple comparisons are rare, so the reader cannot tell whether the added module or run-to-run noise produced the reported gap.

## Cross-Series and Graph-Structured Load Forecasting

A second thread asks how a forecaster should use the fact that regional or nodal load series are correlated. Early multivariate deep models encoded cross-series dependence implicitly: LSTNet [Lai et al., 2018] mixed convolutional and recurrent channels over all series jointly. Explicit graph formulations came from traffic forecasting — spatio-temporal graph convolution over a known sensor network [Yu et al., 2018] — and were freed from the need for a predefined graph by MTGNN [Wu et al., 2020], which learns the adjacency structure end-to-end while forecasting. The power-load instantiations of this idea are now numerous in the target venues: tensor graph convolution over spatio-temporal load characteristics in LoadSeer [Zhang et al., 2024], Bayesian-optimized GCN–BiLSTM–Adaboost stacks [Li et al., 2025], transformer spatio-temporal graph networks for multi-energy load [Zhou et al., 2025], and GCN–Transformer integrations for short-term power load [Wu et al., 2025]. A parallel statistical literature treats the multi-series structure as a *hierarchy* to be reconciled rather than a graph to be learned: optimal combination forecasts for hierarchical time series [Hyndman et al., 2011] and trace-minimization reconciliation [Wickramasuriya et al., 2019] are the standard references, and public datasets with genuine customer/region/system hierarchies — notably the Ausgrid solar-home dataset [Ratnam et al., 2017] — provide the natural testbeds. Across the graph-based load papers, the same evidential pattern as in the first thread recurs with one addition: the papers propose increasingly elaborate mechanisms for *how* cross-series information is weighted (learned adjacency, attention coefficients, tensor decompositions), yet none that we are aware of tests whether the specific weighting mechanism outperforms trivial weighting — equal-weight neighbor averaging — under an identical training protocol with seed-level significance testing. The question "does the graph help?" is systematically conflated with "does *my parameterization* of the graph help?", and only the first is usually answerable from the published tables.

## Simple Baselines and Honest Evaluation

The third thread supplies the discipline. Forecast-evaluation methodology has long warned that impressive-looking gains evaporate under sound protocols: [Tashman, 2000] catalogued the requirements of legitimate out-of-sample testing, [Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012] showed how cross-validation schemes leak temporal information unless splits respect time order, [Hyndman and Koehler, 2006] exposed the failure modes of common accuracy measures (directly relevant here: MAPE degenerates near zero loads), and the Diebold–Mariano test [Diebold and Mariano, 1995] remains the canonical instrument for asking whether two forecasts differ *statistically* rather than numerically. The machine-learning era sharpened the warning into an embarrassment: [Makridakis et al., 2018] found that then-current ML forecasters lost to statistical methods on M3 data, and the M4 competition [Makridakis et al., 2020] showed that the winning entries were hybrids in which the statistical component did essential work. Within deep forecasting itself, [Zeng et al., 2023] asked "are transformers effective for time-series forecasting?" and answered with DLinear — a one-layer linear model that outperformed a generation of transformer designs on standard long-horizon benchmarks, forcing a re-examination of what the elaborate architectures had actually contributed. Best-practice guides now consolidate these lessons for practitioners [Hewamalage et al., 2023]: temporal splits without leakage, multiple runs, significance tests with multiplicity correction, and metrics matched to the data's scale behavior. This thread has, however, mostly operated at *whole-model* granularity — a simple model versus a complex model. Its natural continuation, applying the same statistical severity to the *individual components* of a complex model, is exactly what the hybrid load-forecasting literature of the first two threads lacks.

## Gap Statement

Placing the three threads side by side exposes a specific gap. The graph/cross-series load-forecasting literature proposes ever-richer parameterizations of cross-series weighting but validates them with single-run accuracy tables; the honest-evaluation literature has the statistical machinery (multi-seed distributions, non-parametric tests, Holm correction, leakage-free temporal splits) but has aimed it at whole models, not components; and no prior load-forecasting study we are aware of reports, as a primary finding, that its own weighting mechanism is statistically *inseparable* from trivial alternatives. This paper occupies that intersection. Under a single identical protocol — same splits, same optimizer, same training budget, ten seeds, Mann–Whitney tests with Holm correction — we (i) establish that cross-series attention aggregation contributes significantly to day-ahead multi-region load forecasting on OPSD (against both the strongest external baseline and the no-aggregation ablation), and (ii) report as an honest negative result that the *form* of the aggregation weights — hyperbolic (Poincaré-ball) distance, Euclidean distance, equal-weight neighbors, fixed versus adaptive curvature — is statistically inseparable in all five dataset/horizon settings we test. To our knowledge, component-level significance testing that separates "aggregation helps" from "this particular weighting geometry helps" has not previously been reported for multi-region load forecasting, and the second finding directly cautions the geometry-elaboration trend visible in the second thread.
