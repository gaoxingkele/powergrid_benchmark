Our work sits at the intersection of three research threads: budget-constrained combinatorial optimization and project portfolio selection, investment planning and project review practice in power grids, and local-search-enhanced multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs). We review each thread in turn and then position BiLo-NSGA against them and against its companion method TRACE-MOEA.

## Budget-Constrained Combinatorial Optimization and Project Portfolio Selection

Selecting a subset of candidate items under a budget is the classical multi-objective knapsack setting, and it has served as a canonical stress test for evolutionary multi-objective optimization since [Zitzler and Thiele, 1999] used it to compare Pareto-based selection schemes. Two lessons from this literature are directly relevant to project review. First, plain dominance-based evolution struggles on knapsack-type landscapes unless it is combined with problem-aware search: [Jaszkiewicz, 2002] showed that a genetic algorithm hybridized with local search over scalarizing functions substantially outperforms purely recombinative MOEAs on multi-objective knapsack instances, a finding later systematized in the survey of [Lust and Teghem, 2012]. Second, the difficulty compounds as objectives accumulate; [Ishibuchi et al., 2015] demonstrated that the behavior of standard MOEAs degrades in characteristic ways on many-objective knapsack problems, which cautions against adopting an off-the-shelf algorithm for multi-criteria selection tasks. The project portfolio community absorbed these lessons early. [Doerner et al., 2004] applied Pareto ant colony optimization to multiobjective portfolio selection precisely because exact methods could not scale to realistic candidate pools, and [Carazo et al., 2010] formulated a comprehensive portfolio model with project interdependencies, scheduling, and resource limits, solving it with a metaheuristic tailored to feasibility handling. In the energy domain, this thread surfaces as investment-portfolio models with coupled risk and benefit objectives [Gao et al., 2023] and, more recently, as hybrid exact-heuristic schemes for investment planning when cost functions resist analytical treatment [Xiong et al., 2026]. What the portfolio thread has not produced, however, is an operator design in which the budget constraint itself shapes the neighborhood structure of the search: budgets are typically enforced by penalty terms or repair heuristics that act after variation, rather than by moves that are defined in budget-aware project terms (add under slack, remove under violation, substitute under parity).

## Power Grid Investment Planning and Project Review Decision Methods

Power grid investment decisions have traditionally been studied either as expansion-planning optimization or as multi-criteria review problems. On the optimization side, recent target-venue work formulates multi-stage coordinated planning of transmission and storage under large-scale renewable integration [Liang et al., 2024], quantifies the value of reliability investment through interruption-cost estimation [Bhattarai and Karki, 2024], and analyzes investment return of grid-side storage against multi-dimensional security requirements [Zhang et al., 2025]. These works optimize the composition or sizing of a specific asset class, but the reviewed alternatives are usually few and hand-constructed; the combinatorial explosion of choosing among more than a hundred heterogeneous candidate projects is out of their scope. On the review side, a long multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) tradition anchored in AHP [Saaty, 1980] and TOPSIS [Hwang and Yoon, 1981] remains the de facto standard in grid project appraisal, and it is still actively extended: [Mizrak and Yasar, 2026] recently combined objective criteria weighting with explainability layers for strategic energy investment prioritization. MCDM scoring is attractive to review boards because every ranking step is auditable, yet it evaluates projects independently and therefore cannot capture portfolio-level effects such as budget crowding-out or inter-project dependencies. A complementary line assesses grid projects through simulation, for example the quantitative evaluation of crucial substations and the system-level impact of commissioning delays in multi-voltage planning [Lu et al., 2025], which underlines that review verdicts must reflect network context rather than per-project scores. In short, the grid literature offers either portfolio-blind but auditable scoring, or portfolio-aware but audit-opaque optimization; a method that keeps the auditability of the MCDM tradition while searching the combinatorial portfolio space has been missing.

## Local-Search-Enhanced Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms

Hybridizing evolutionary search with local refinement, often called memetic search, is one of the oldest and most robust ideas in multi-objective optimization. [Ishibuchi and Murata, 1998] introduced the multi-objective genetic local search template, applying weighted-sum local moves after genetic variation, and [Knowles and Corne, 2000] showed that even a (1+1) local search with a Pareto archive can approximate nondominated fronts competitively. The dominant modern frameworks — NSGA-II [Deb et al., 2002], MOEA/D [Zhang and Li, 2007], and NSGA-III [Deb and Jain, 2014] — are, by contrast, driven by selection pressure and recombination alone, which is why a large body of follow-up work grafts local search onto them; [Neri and Cotta, 2012] survey the design space of such memetic combinations. In the power systems venues we target, this hybridization impulse is visible but generic: improved NSGA-II variants for distribution network co-optimization [He et al., 2025], NSGA-II for offshore wind storage configuration [Lin et al., 2025], staged NSGA-II schemes for resilient backbone-grid planning [Ding et al., 2026], and enhanced swarm metaheuristics for storage planning [Qi et al., 2025] or transmission expansion [Demirbas et al., 2025] all modify initialization, operators, or stages, yet their local moves (when present) are numeric perturbations inherited from continuous benchmarks. None of these hybrids defines local search in the vocabulary of the downstream decision — adding, removing, or substituting a reviewable project — and none records the move sequence itself as an output. This matters for review applications specifically: a search trajectory expressed as domain moves is exactly the artifact a review board needs to audit why a portfolio was recommended, and discarding it forfeits the main advantage a metaheuristic could offer over black-box scoring.

## Relation to TRACE-MOEA and Gap Statement

A companion study developed in our broader research program, TRACE-MOEA [companion], addresses power grid project review with a different mechanism and a different problem framing, and we delineate the two explicitly. Mechanistically, TRACE-MOEA layers a preference-adaptive ranking mechanism over an NSGA-II core, complemented by review-rule-driven repair of candidate portfolios and a trace archive that records ranking decisions; its contribution lives in the selection and ranking stage. BiLo-NSGA instead retains a single-population non-dominated sorting kernel and concentrates its contribution in the variation stage: a bidirectional local search whose forward pass inserts projects under budget slack, whose backward pass removes projects under violation or dominance pressure, and whose substitution moves exchange projects at budget parity, with dependency-aware move bonuses and feasibility recovery as separate switchable components. In problem setting, TRACE-MOEA targets investment effectiveness and decision traceability at the ranking level, whereas this paper treats the budget as a first-class hard constraint — the experimental axes (budget sensitivity scans, tightened and loosened budget regimes) are organized around how portfolio quality responds to the budget envelope, a question the sibling work does not pose. The two studies share only the public benchmark-derivation pipeline for candidate projects, which we document in the Data Availability statement.

Against all three threads, the gap this paper fills is specific: to our knowledge, no prior work embeds a bidirectional, project-vocabulary local search (forward insertion, backward removal, budget-parity substitution) inside a non-dominated sorting framework for budget-hard-constrained grid project portfolio review, while emitting a per-move audit trail that makes each accepted change to the portfolio individually inspectable. Existing knapsack MOEAs treat the budget as a penalty or repair afterthought; grid MCDM review methods are auditable but portfolio-blind; and memetic MOEAs refine numerically rather than in reviewable decision terms. BiLo-NSGA is designed to close precisely this intersection.
